# A start-up course that teaches the way people actually learn

*September 2026. What the evidence says about learning a skill, what the
canon says defines a good start-up, and the course those two things
produce. Sources at the end of each part.*

## Part one: how people learn a skill

### The two techniques with the evidence, and the five without

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013) reviewed ten study
techniques against the evidence and rated them. Two came out high on
every measure — they work for different ages, abilities, materials and
tests, and they work in real classrooms, not only labs:

- **Practice testing.** Being asked to recall or apply a thing, and
  finding out whether you were right, beats reading it again by a wide
  margin. The act of trying to retrieve is what builds the memory.
- **Distributed practice.** The same total time spread over days beats
  one sitting. Spacing forces retrieval after partial forgetting, which
  is exactly the retrieval that works.

Three were rated moderate — promising, less evidence in classrooms:
**interleaving** (mixing kinds of problems rather than blocking them),
**elaborative interrogation** (asking "why is this true?") and
**self-explanation** (saying how a new thing connects to what you know).

Five were rated low, and they are the five most courses are made of:
summarising, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text, and
**rereading**. A course that is mostly text to read, however good the
text, is built on the technique that works least.

### What Duolingo does with this

Duolingo's own research (Settles & Meeder, 2016) built its scheduling on
the same finding: each item has a half-life in memory that shrinks with
each miss and grows with each success, and the app brings an item back
when it is about to be forgotten. Their published model (half-life
regression) is trained on millions of learner traces; a small course
cannot do that, but it can use the shape: a widening schedule, reset by
a mistake.

The structure around it, as of the 2023–2026 path redesign: one path,
not a tree, so two learners who did the same work are in the same place;
short thematic units with a guidebook each, for reference rather than
study; review levels spread through the path rather than stacked at the
end; a practice hub that resurfaces mistakes; and a "jump here" test at
the start of any later unit, so someone who already knows the material
is not made to sit through it.

What Duolingo also does, and this course does not: hearts that run out
and stop you learning, streaks that reset to zero, leagues that demote
you, and paid repairs for all three. The research in
`what-people-want.md` found being punished for an absence is the single
most-named reason people delete a habit app, and the product brief for
the journey forbids all four. They are not the learning; they are the
retention mechanics around it, and the retention they buy is the kind
that ends in a deletion.

### The course's rules, from the above

1. **A lesson is short to read and long to do.** Two to four short
   paragraphs, then four to six exercises with a right answer the
   learner can be wrong about. The exercises are the lesson.
2. **Every exercise explains its answer.** The "why" is where
   elaborative interrogation lives: the learner reads why the wrong one
   is wrong, not only that it is.
3. **Old exercises come back on a widening schedule** (1, 3, 7, 14, 30
   days), and a miss puts the exercise back to the front. Practice
   sessions draw from what is due, mistakes first, mixed across units.
4. **Later lessons reuse earlier concepts** in their exercises, so a
   unit-four question about pricing asks about the customer conversation
   from unit two. That is the interleaving.
5. **Every lesson ends in the learner's own start-up.** The apply step
   writes one line to their idea page. Transfer to the real thing is the
   point of the course, and it is practised every lesson rather than
   promised at the end.
6. **Two passes.** Basics lessons teach the concept and the rule of
   thumb; depth lessons teach the number, where the rule bends, and what
   people get wrong. A learner who is early does the basics through the
   whole road before any depth; a learner who is further along takes a
   unit's checkpoint and jumps.
7. **Nothing punishes.** No lives, no streak, no league. Points are for
   learning, are modest, and are the same whether the learner's own
   assumption survived the lesson or died in it.

## Part two: what defines a good start-up

The course teaches the things that separate start-ups that work from
ones that do not. That is a specific list, and it is shorter than most
start-up content suggests.

### Why they die

CB Insights' post-mortem analysis (updated through 2025) is the most
cited count of causes, and its top line has not moved in a decade: about
**42% failed because there was no market need** for what they built.
Running out of cash (29%) is usually the final cause rather than the
root one — the money ran out because nobody needed the thing. Then: not
the right team (23%), outcompeted, pricing and cost problems (18%), a
poor product, no business model, poor marketing, ignoring customers, bad
timing.

So the course spends its longest stretch — two of six sections — on
finding out whether anyone needs the thing, before it spends a lesson
on building it. That order is the whole argument of the course.

### What a start-up is

Paul Graham's definition is the one this course uses because it is the
one with a consequence: **a start-up is a company designed to grow
fast.** Not "a new company", not "a tech company". A café is a fine
business and not a start-up. The consequence is that everything about
one — the idea, the team, the money — is chosen for growth, and a
founder who does not want that should know it early and build something
else, happily.

Steve Blank's definition is the other half: **a start-up is a temporary
organisation searching for a repeatable business model.** Not executing
a plan — searching for one. Customer development (discovery, validation,
creation, company building) is the search, and "get out of the
building" is its method: the answers are with customers, not in the
office.

### The idea

Graham again: the best ideas are things the founders themselves want,
can build, and that few others see as worth doing. Ideas come from
noticing problems, especially your own, not from trying to think of
ideas. "Why now" — a recent change in the world that makes this possible
when it was not two years ago — is what separates an idea whose time has
come from one that many have tried. And a small market that is growing
fast beats a big flat one, because the small one's edge can be defended.

### The customer conversation

Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* is the standard because it is short and
it is right. Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask
about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future; talk less.
Three kinds of bad data: compliments, fluff (generics, hypotheticals,
"I would definitely"), and ideas. A good meeting ends in a commitment —
time, reputation or money — or an advancement to a next step; a meeting
that ends in "sounds great, keep me posted" was a failure that felt like
a success.

### The product, and product-market fit

Build the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption: a manual
service, a demonstration, a landing page with a price on it, a
pre-order. **Do things that don't scale** (Graham, 2013): recruit the
first users one at a time, by hand, and give them an experience no
scaled company could — Airbnb's founders photographing listings, Stripe's
founders setting up accounts on the customer's own laptop.

Product-market fit has two honest measures. Sean Ellis's survey: ask
users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product; if
40% or more say "very disappointed", you have it. And the retention
curve: plot the share of a cohort still active week by week; if the
curve flattens above zero, some people have made the product a habit; if
it falls to zero, nobody has. Rahul Vohra's PMF engine at Superhuman
turned the survey into a method — segment to the users who answered
"very disappointed", find what they love, find what holds the
"somewhat disappointed" back, split the roadmap between the two — and
moved the score from 22% to 58% in a year.

### The money

Kevin Hale's pricing lecture at YC: the commonest mistake is charging
too little. Price on value, not cost. Companies that charge more can
fund distribution and product, and grow faster. Unit economics are one
customer's arithmetic: what it costs to get one (CAC), what one is worth
over their life (LTV), and how many months until they have paid back
their cost. Working benchmarks: LTV at least three times CAC, payback
under twelve months. And the founder's own number, from Graham's
"Default Alive or Default Dead": if spending stays flat and revenue keeps
growing at its recent rate, do you reach profitability before the cash
runs out? Every founder should know that answer every month.

### Growth

Weinberg and Mares's *Traction* lists nineteen channels and the
Bullseye method for choosing among them: brainstorm every channel, rank
by likely impact, test the top three cheaply and in parallel, then focus
on the one that works. Before any of that, the first customers come by
hand. Adora Cheung at YC on metrics: one primary number, revenue or
active users, set weekly, because a start-up needs feedback every week;
YC's benchmark for a good weekly growth rate is 5–7%. Dave McClure's
AARRR (acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue) is the
frame for where a number goes wrong.

### The team

YC's advice on founders, against the common advice: choose someone you
would want to be friends with; similar people get on, and getting on
matters more than complementary skills; ask whether this person would
start a good company with or without you. Split equity equally or close
to it; vest over four years with a one-year cliff, even as a solo
founder.

### The company

The boring hour: an entity, a business account, books done monthly by a
named person or tool, founder agreements with vesting, a domain and a
real email. None of it is the business, and skipping it is how the
terrible month happens. And the founder's week: three tasks, one
number, a Friday when it is read back — the rhythm the rest of
FounderFloor already runs on.

## Part three: the course

Six sections. The order is the argument: what a start-up is, then the
customer, then the product, then the money, then growth, then the
company. The first three come before building anything because that is
where 42% of the deaths are.

| Section | Units |
|---|---|
| 1 What a start-up is | What a start-up is · What makes an idea good · Founders |
| 2 The customer | The problem · Talking to people · Evidence |
| 3 The product | The smallest thing · Testing an offer · Product-market fit |
| 4 The money | Pricing · One customer's arithmetic · Runway |
| 5 Growth | The first customers, by hand · Channels · The one number |
| 6 The company | The boring hour · A founder's week · Raise or bootstrap |

Each unit: a one-page guide, three basics lessons, one depth lesson, a
checkpoint. Each lesson: one concept, two to four paragraphs, an
example, four to six exercises, one line to remember, and an apply step
that writes to the learner's idea page. Where a unit has real-world
work — a conversation, a test — it points at the journey mission that
records it, so the course and the missions are one road.

The existing ten missions stay as the road's real-world spine; the
course is what you learn on the way to each of them.

## What this does not claim

Completing the course does not make a start-up succeed, and no lesson
says it does. What it does is put the founder's attention on the things
the evidence says decide the outcome — need, retention, unit economics,
runway — before the things that feel like progress and are not.
